## Option A — Question: "How do I calculate my freelance hourly rate?"

**Post this as an answer:**

---

Most freelancers calculate their rate by dividing desired income by 2,080 hours (a full work year). That number is almost always wrong.

The problem: 2,080 assumes you are billing every single working hour. You are not. Client work takes maybe 50-60% of your time. The rest is admin, sales, marketing, revisions, bookkeeping, and dead time between projects.

**The real calculation:**

1. **Start with your target annual income** — what you need to take home after taxes
2. **Add taxes** — self-employment tax in the US is 15.3% on top of income tax. A rough multiplier: divide take-home by 0.65 for a solo freelancer in a moderate tax bracket
3. **Add overhead** — software, equipment, insurance, co-working, professional development
4. **Calculate your billable hours** — not 2,080. Track for a month and see. Most freelancers bill 800-1,200 hours per year
5. **Add a profit margin** — 15-20% minimum for business stability and slow periods

**Example:**

- Target take-home: $60,000
- Gross needed (after tax): ~$92,000
- Overhead: $8,000/year
- Total needed: $100,000
- Billable hours (realistic): 1,000/year
- Rate before profit: $100/hr
- With 20% margin: $120/hr

Most people doing this manually undercount overhead and overcount billable hours — both push the rate down. [FreelanceRateWise](https://freelanceratewise.com) runs all of this in one calculator, including non-billable hours, tax rate, and overhead breakdown. Free, no account.

The uncomfortable truth: if you have been charging $50-75/hr and this math says $110+, you are subsidizing your clients.

## Option B — Question: "Why do so many freelancers undercharge?"

**Post this as an answer:**

---

A few reasons that compound each other:

**1. They calculate rate wrong.**
Most freelancers divide annual income target by hours in a work year (2,080). That assumes 100% of your time is billable. In practice it is closer to 50-60%. The rest is admin, proposals, revisions, outreach, and downtime between projects.

Doubling the denominator error means the rate comes out roughly half of what it should be.

**2. They forget taxes.**
When you are employed, taxes come out before you see the money. When you freelance, you are responsible for both the employee and employer sides of payroll tax (self-employment tax in the US is 15.3%). Many new freelancers set a rate based on their old salary, not accounting for the fact that $80k freelance is worth significantly less than $80k employed.

**3. They forget overhead.**
Software subscriptions, hardware, health insurance (if self-paying), professional development, accountant fees, business insurance. These can add $5,000-$15,000/year to the true cost of operating.

**4. They anchor to market rates without checking the math.**
They see competitors charging $60-80/hr and match it without verifying those rates cover their own costs.

The fix is running the actual numbers. [FreelanceRateWise](https://freelanceratewise.com) is a free calculator that factors in non-billable hours, tax rate, overhead, and profit margin to give you a survival rate (break-even) and a growth rate (where you actually get ahead). No sign-up.

The number you get is usually higher than what most freelancers charge. That is the point.

## Option C — Question: "How many billable hours does a freelancer actually work per year?"

**Post this as an answer:**

---

Far fewer than people assume.

A full-time employment year is ~2,080 hours. A realistic freelance billable year is closer to 800-1,200 hours for most solo practitioners. Here is why:

- **Client acquisition:** Writing proposals, discovery calls, follow-ups. Unpaid.
- **Admin:** Invoicing, contracts, bookkeeping, taxes. Unpaid.
- **Marketing:** Content, networking, maintaining a portfolio. Unpaid.
- **Revisions and scope creep:** Often partially unpaid.
- **Gaps between projects:** Unavoidable, especially early on.
- **Vacation, sick days:** No one is covering these.

If you bill 25 hours per week consistently across 48 working weeks, that is 1,200 billable hours. That is a strong year for most freelancers, not a bad one.

This matters enormously for rate setting. If you divide your income target by 2,080, your rate is roughly half of what it should be. Divide by 1,000-1,200 and you get a more honest number.
