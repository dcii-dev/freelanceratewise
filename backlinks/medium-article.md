## Article: Why Your Freelance Hourly Rate Is Probably Wrong (And How to Fix It)

**Suggested tags:** Freelancing, Business, Personal Finance, Self Employment, Career

---

Most freelancers set their rate by one of two methods: they look at what competitors charge, or they divide their desired annual income by 2,080. Both methods produce a number that is too low.

Here is the math that actually works.

### The 2,080 Hour Problem

A standard employment year is 52 weeks at 40 hours per week. 2,080 hours. When freelancers calculate their rate, many divide their target income by this number.

The problem: freelancers do not bill 2,080 hours per year. Not even close.

Consider everything that does not get invoiced:

- Writing proposals and scope documents
- Discovery calls and pre-sales conversations
- Admin: invoicing, contracts, bookkeeping
- Chasing late payments
- Marketing: portfolio updates, content, networking
- Revisions that go beyond scope
- Gaps between projects
- Vacation and sick days — no one covers these

For a solo freelancer working seriously, 800-1,200 billable hours per year is realistic. 1,400+ is exceptional. If you are using 2,080 as your denominator, your rate is roughly half of what it should be.

### The Tax Layer Most People Miss

When you are employed, taxes are withheld before you see a dollar. When you freelance, you are responsible for all of it — and in the US, self-employment tax is 15.3% on top of income tax. That covers both the employee and employer sides of Social Security and Medicare.

A freelancer targeting $70,000 take-home needs to earn closer to $105,000-$115,000 gross, depending on their state and deductions.

Many new freelancers set a rate based on their old salary without accounting for this. The result is a rate that feels competitive but leaves them behind.

### Overhead Is a Real Number

Software subscriptions, hardware replacement, health insurance (if self-paying), professional liability insurance, accountant fees, professional development, co-working space. These are real costs of running a freelance business.

Add them up for a year. For most freelancers the total is $5,000-$15,000 annually. That number needs to come out of revenue before you reach your take-home target.

### The Two Numbers You Actually Need

**Survival rate:** The rate at which you break even — covering taxes, overhead, and personal income at the minimum you need to operate. This is your floor. Going below it means you are losing money.

**Growth rate:** The rate that covers everything above, plus a profit margin (15-20% minimum) for saving, slow periods, investing in the business, and building runway. This is your real target rate.

Most freelancers know neither number.

### Running the Calculation

The formula:

```
gross needed = (target take-home + overhead) / (1 - effective tax rate)
hourly rate  = gross needed / realistic billable hours
growth rate  = hourly rate * (1 + profit margin)
```

Example (US freelancer, moderate tax bracket):

- Target take-home: $65,000
- Overhead: $8,000
- Effective tax rate: 28%
- Gross needed: ($65,000 + $8,000) / 0.72 = $101,389
- Realistic billable hours: 1,000/year
- Base rate: $101/hr
- Growth rate (20% margin): $121/hr

### What to Do With the Number

If the calculator produces a rate significantly higher than what you are currently charging:

1. **Do not immediately raise rates on existing clients.** Adjust gradually or on renewal.
2. **Raise rates on new clients immediately.** You are not obligated to offer new clients your old rate.
3. **Track actual billable hours for 30 days.** The calculator is only as accurate as your inputs. Real data beats estimates.
4. **Revisit overhead annually.** Costs change. Your rate should reflect current costs, not what you estimated three years ago.

The rate that feels competitive is usually the rate that keeps you on a treadmill. The rate that feels a little uncomfortable is usually closer to correct.
